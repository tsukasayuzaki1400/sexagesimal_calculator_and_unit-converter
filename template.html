    About these numerals

    ~4000 years ago Babylonians wrote all numbers in base 60 — the system behind our 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle. A vertical wedge is 1, a corner wedge (𒌋) is 10; ones stack in rows of three after the tens. They had no zero, so an empty place is marked with a pair of slanted wedges. The calculator uses the authentic Unicode cuneiform number signs when your device has a cuneiform font, and draws them otherwise.

    How it works

    • Positional, like ours. Each place is worth 60× the one to its right, exactly as decimal places are worth 10×. The digits themselves run from 1 to 59.
    • Two signs build every digit. A vertical wedge = 1, the corner wedge 𒌋 = 10. So 𒌋𒌋𒐂 reads 20 + 4 = 24; unit wedges never exceed three per row — 9 (𒐇) stacks in three rows.
    • Places are read left to right, most significant first: 1,25 means 1×60 + 25 = 85. Tap the digit pad here the same way: tap 1 then 25 and you'll see 1,25.
    • Fractions worked the same way. Places right of the radix point count sixtieths: 0;30 = 30/60 = one half. Babylonian scribes did division by multiplying by reciprocals — handy with so many exact fractions available.

    Why base 60?

    Sixty is divisible by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30 — more than any smaller number. Halves, thirds, quarters and fifths all come out exact, which made trade, land measurement and astronomy painless. The Sumerians started counting in sixties around 3000 BCE; Old Babylonian scribes (~1900–1600 BCE) turned it into the fully positional system this calculator mimics. "Cuneiform" just means wedge-shaped — scribes pressed reed styluses into wet clay tablets that then dried nearly indestructible, which is why millions of their homework sheets still survive.

    The missing zero

    For centuries an empty place was simply left blank, so "1,1" (3600+1) could be misread as "11". Later scribes began filling gaps with two slanted wedges — the world's earliest placeholder zero. It was never treated as a number you can calculate with; that idea had to wait for Indian mathematicians over two thousand years later. The dashed key on the pad plays the same role.

    The legacy on your clock

    When Greek astronomers adopted Babylonian data, they kept the sixtieths: a degree split into 60 partes minutae primae ("first small parts") and again into secundae. That's literally where minutes and seconds come from — on maps and on your watch face. This app's °′″ and h m s result modes write numbers exactly the way those astronomers would have.
